1. Houthi missiles hit Riyadh's main airport, killing at least 12

Qué pasó

On 10 October 2026, a Houthi missile strike hit a passenger terminal at King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, killing at least 12 people and injuring more than 300. A second projectile hit Terminal 4 the next day, killing one more person and wounding 11. Saudi Arabia suspended operations, schools in the capital moved online, and the Saudi-led coalition answered with airstrikes on Sanaa. The strikes came days after Yemen's government launched a major offensive against the Houthis, and they pulled the Saudi capital directly into the wider regional war around Iran.

Sources: Democracy Now! headlines (12 Oct 2026), Just Security Early Edition (12 Oct 2026), BBC: Aramco Tower hit, The National (8 Nov 2026), Al Jazeera (10 Nov 2026), AeroCorner: airport status

Conexión con hoy

The airport was closed for about two weeks and partly reopened on 25 October with a reduced schedule. Attacks haven't stopped: on 7–8 November the Houthis targeted the airport, Aramco sites and Riyadh's financial district, and BBC Verify footage shows the Aramco Tower was hit harder than the Saudi energy ministry's "minor damage" statement suggested. On 10 November the coalition said it shot down more missiles fired at Riyadh. Etihad, Lufthansa, Air India, flydubai and others still have Riyadh flights suspended into mid- or late November. The UN-brokered truce that had mostly held since 2022 is effectively gone.

    2. Trump declares a Russia–Ukraine "energy ceasefire" after a diesel deal with Putin

    Qué pasó

    On 11 October 2026, Donald Trump posted that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an immediate "energy ceasefire" and warned both sides "Do not break it!" Zelensky said the announcement was "news to me," and Russia hit a power plant in Kharkiv the same day. Two days earlier Trump had announced that Putin would release Russian diesel onto world markets, and the U.S. Treasury issued a license suspending sanctions on Russian diesel through 7 April 2027. Zelensky called it a "gift to Putin." Trump also said it was "time for Ukraine to get a new president."

    Sources: AP News, BBC: diesel deal, NBC News / Reuters (1 Nov 2026), Interfax-Ukraine (10 Nov 2026), Kyiv Independent: Abu Dhabi talks

    Conexión con hoy

    There is no working energy truce. Ukraine briefly paused strikes on Russian refineries under U.S. pressure, then resumed them; Russia keeps hitting Ukraine's grid, and on 10 November emergency blackouts were in force in Kyiv, Kharkiv, Poltava and Sumy. Trump also waived a package of new Russia sanctions, which strained U.S.–Ukraine relations. One real development: U.S., Ukrainian and Russian delegations restarted trilateral peace talks in Abu Dhabi on 10 November, the first since February, with an energy and maritime ceasefire on Kyiv's agenda.

      3. A magnitude 7.7 earthquake strikes central Panama

      Qué pasó

      On 9 October 2026, a magnitude 7.7 earthquake struck near Pitaloza Arriba in Herrera province, at a shallow depth of about 12.6 km. Footage showed a cathedral collapsing and a hotel facade falling onto a car in Penonomé. More than 600 aftershocks followed. The government confirmed one death, 68-year-old Elena Barría, and 27 injuries. Hospitals in the Azuero peninsula were damaged and the Tonosí rural hospital was knocked out of service.

      Sources: AP (Spanish), CCRIF event briefing (PDF), La Prensa Panamá: one month later, Euronews (9 Oct 2026)

      Conexión con hoy

      The low death toll held, but the housing damage turned out to be huge. By 5 November, authorities had evaluated 19,865 homes: 4,633 were destroyed and 15,740 need some repair. About 3,356 people were still in 113 shelters at the start of November. Only 20 to 25 new homes had been finished by 8 November, and President José Raúl Mulino has promised 2,700 housing solutions by March 2027, which means about 75 a week. Panama's catastrophe insurance with the Caribbean facility CCRIF triggered a payout.

        1. The BBC's director-general resigns over the Panorama Trump edit

        Qué pasó

The BBC is trying to get it dismissed, arguing that Panorama was never broadcast or available in the U.S. Trump has been ordered to answer questions under oath, and a trial is set for February 2027 if the case survives. Former Google executive Matt Brittin was named the new director-general and took office on 18 May 2026.

          2. Syria's Ahmed al-Sharaa visits the White House

          Qué pasó

          On 10 November 2025, Ahmed al-Sharaa became the first Syrian head of state to visit the White House. Less than a year earlier he had led the rebel offensive that toppled Bashar al-Assad, and he had once been a designated terrorist with a U.S. bounty on his head. Trump promised to do "everything" he could to help Syria succeed, and Washington extended a suspension of the Caesar Act sanctions as Syria moved to join the U.S.-led coalition against ISIS.

          Sources: Reuters (10 Nov 2025), Al-Monitor: Caesar repeal vote, Al-Monitor (May 2026), Alhurra: U.S. bets on Syria

          Conexión con hoy

          The turnaround stuck. Congress repealed the Caesar Act in December 2025, and in August 2026 the U.S. took Syria off its list of state sponsors of terrorism, removing major barriers to investment. Al-Sharaa remains president, but Syria still faces instability, corruption and security threats, and analysts note that Damascus keeps working ties with Russia even as it leans toward Washington.

            3. COP30 opens in Belém, at the edge of the Amazon

            Qué pasó

            On 10 November 2025, the UN climate summit COP30 opened in Belém, Brazil, the first held in the Amazon region. The United States sent no official delegation. Brazil pushed for a roadmap to move away from fossil fuels and launched a fund for standing tropical forests, while Indigenous protesters broke into the venue to demand a voice. The final deal tripled adaptation finance by 2035 but left out any negotiated fossil-fuel roadmap after resistance from oil producers.

            Sources: UN News: COP31 opens in Antalya, COP30 Presidency: from Belém to Antalya, Carbon Brief Q&A on the roadmap, UNFCCC: COP31

            Conexión con hoy

            Because the talks failed to agree on one, Brazil's presidency published its own voluntary, non-binding roadmap for transitioning away from fossil fuels. COP31 opened in Antalya, Türkiye, on 9 November 2026 and runs to 20 November. Its formal agenda centres on electrification, including a target of 35% of global final energy coming from electricity by 2035, and the Brazilian roadmap is being promoted there as an implementation tool rather than a binding decision.

              1. Ortega wins a fourth straight term in a vote with no real rivals

              Qué pasó

              On 7 November 2021, Daniel Ortega was declared winner of Nicaragua's presidential election with about 76% of the vote, with his wife Rosario Murillo again as vice-president. In the months before the vote his government jailed seven would-be opposition candidates and dozens of activists, journalists and business leaders, and banned the main opposition parties. The U.S., the EU and the UK called the election a sham, and Washington threatened new sanctions.

              Sources: Reuters (8 Nov 2021), AP News, EFE (10 Nov 2026), Confidencial, AP: UN experts on crimes against humanity

              Conexión con hoy

              The reporting was accurate, and the slide into one-family rule deepened. Nicaragua expelled 222 political prisoners to the U.S. in 2023 and stripped them of citizenship, and a 2025 constitutional reform made Ortega and Murillo "co-presidents" and extended their hold over every branch of government. UN experts say the couple may have committed crimes against humanity. Ortega, now 80, reappeared in public on 10 November 2026 after a 66-day absence that had fed speculation about his health.

                    1. India withdraws 86% of its cash overnight

                    Qué pasó

                    On 8 November 2016, Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced in a televised address that ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes, about 86% of the currency in circulation, would stop being legal tender at midnight. The stated goals were to wipe out black money, fake notes and terrorist financing. The result was weeks of bank queues, cash shortages and disruption for farmers, small traders and daily-wage workers.

                    Sources: Indian Express: 10 years of demonetisation, DW: promises remain unfulfilled, The Hindu: cash usage unchanged, fake notes rising, Scroll

                    Conexión con hoy

                    On the tenth anniversary, the verdict is mostly negative. The Reserve Bank of India later reported that about 99% of the banned notes came back into the banks, which undercut the black-money goal. Cash in circulation has more than doubled since before the ban, and fake notes are rising again. The lasting change is the boom in digital payments through UPI, India's instant payment system, though analysts disagree on how much demonetisation caused it. Modi remains prime minister.

                      2. Colombia and the FARC announce a revised peace deal

                      Qué pasó

                      On 12 November 2016, six weeks after voters narrowly rejected the original accord in a referendum, Colombia's government and the FARC guerrillas announced a revised agreement in Havana. It included changes demanded by the "No" camp, such as an inventory of FARC assets for victim reparations and tighter rules on the special peace tribunal. President Juan Manuel Santos and FARC leader Rodrigo Londoño, known as "Timochenko," signed it on 24 November at Bogotá's Teatro Colón, and Congress, not another referendum, ratified it.

                      Sources: NPR (12 Nov 2016), Reuters (2016), Kroc Institute report (2026), Pares: "Diez años después" (PDF, Spanish), NPR: De la Espriella sworn in

                      Conexión con hoy

                      The FARC disarmed and became a political party, now called Comunes, and thousands of ex-fighters went through reintegration. But ten years on, Notre Dame's Kroc Institute finds only 36% of the 578 commitments fully met and 43% barely started or not started. Rural reform, the safety of former fighters and reparations for victims are still lagging, and dissident groups keep fighting. The new president, the right-wing Abelardo de la Espriella, took office on 7 August 2026 and says he will "rigorously" review the special peace tribunal, the JEP, while respecting the law. Santos and Londoño are both alive.

                        3. The Kaikōura earthquake rips through New Zealand's South Island

                        Qué pasó

                        Just after midnight on 14 November 2016, a magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck near Kaikōura. It ruptured more than 20 faults, one of the most complex quakes ever recorded, lifted parts of the coastline by several metres, and caused landslides that cut off Kaikōura by road and rail. Two people died. Wellington's waterfront and several buildings were badly damaged.

                        Sources: Kaikōura District Council: 10-year anniversary, Engineering NZ: "Moving mountains", Newswire NZ: the faults that broke (2026)

                        Conexión con hoy

                        State Highway 1 north of Kaikōura reopened in December 2017 after a huge rebuild of road and rail, and the quake changed how scientists model multi-fault earthquakes. Kaikōura is marking the tenth anniversary with community events from 12 to 27 November 2026, including a main commemoration on 14 November and a "Resilient by Nature" exhibition.
