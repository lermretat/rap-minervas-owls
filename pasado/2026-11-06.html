1. Yemen launches “Dawn of Yemen” offensive against the Houthis

Qué pasó

On 4 October 2026, Rashad al-Alimi, head of Yemen’s Saudi-backed Presidential Leadership Council, announced Operation Dawn of Yemen—a nationwide campaign to retake Houthi-held territory, including pressure toward Sanaa. The move followed a Houthi lightning advance that seized Mocha and strategic stretches of the Bab al-Mandeb Red Sea coast, a chokepoint that had grown even more critical while the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted. Within a day the Saudi-led coalition said roughly 100 fighter jets were supporting the offensive; Houthis answered with claimed missile and drone strikes on Saudi Aramco sites.

Sources: Al Jazeera, The Guardian, NBC News / Reuters, Al Jazeera (attrition update)

Conexión con hoy

A month on, Al Jazeera and other outlets describe a war of attrition rather than a decisive breakthrough. Government forces report gains in al-Jawf and around Bab al-Mandeb; Houthis have advanced in parts of Taiz and Lahij and keep firing missiles into Saudi Arabia. Sanaa remains under Houthi control amid recurring airstrikes. Early claims of rapid liberation have not been borne out; the fight is now locked into a costly regional proxy struggle with no ceasefire in sight as of early November 2026.

    2. Eritrean troops cross into Ethiopia’s Tigray as war risk spikes

    Qué pasó

    In early October 2026 (BBC reporting peaked around 7–9 October), residents and monitors said Eritrean forces had advanced tens of kilometres into Ethiopia’s Tigray region, including Adigrat, after fighting resumed between Addis Ababa and the Tigray People’s Liberation Front. Ethiopia’s foreign minister told the UN Security Council it faced an “all-out invasion” and reserved the right of self-defence; Eritrea accused Ethiopia of seeking a pretext to seize a Red Sea port. Ethiopian drone strikes hit Adigrat; witnesses described Eritrean vehicles withdrawing north. The flare-up revived fears of a full Horn of Africa war after the 2018 rapprochement had already collapsed.

    Sources: BBC, BBC (deeper troop movements), Al Jazeera, AP

    Conexión con hoy

    By early November 2026, Ethiopian federal forces had retaken major Tigray towns including Mekelle, Shire and Sheraro; Eritrean units largely pulled back toward the border, but ACLED and Crisis Group still flag an active, unstable front. Abiy Ahmed remains in power; Asmara and Addis remain mutually hostile. Early “invasion” framing was partly borne out by troop presence, but a wider interstate war was contained short of all-out conflict—leaving a dangerous frozen crisis rather than peace.

      3. French high-school protests shut hundreds of lycées

      Qué pasó

      Around 1–5 October 2026, a nationwide lycée revolt over teacher shortages, overcrowded classes, dilapidated buildings and long school days peaked when Education Minister Édouard Geffray said 400–500 schools would be partly or fully closed on 5 October; Interior figures put thousands of arrests (mostly minors) and dozens of schools burned or ransacked. Clashes with riot police spread from Paris to Marseille, Nantes and Lyon. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu’s government scrambled with substitute teachers, facility pledges and talk of Parcoursup reform as Macron’s final presidential year approached.

      Sources: BBC, The Guardian, France 24, AP

      Conexión con hoy

      Street intensity has fallen, but the grievance has not. By early November 2026 only about twenty lycées were still blocked; the government has pledged to replace Parcoursup by 2028, add posts and cap class sizes. Student unions call the package insufficient. Early coverage of mass closures and youth-led unrest was accurate; lasting impact is a live education-funding fight heading into the 2027 presidential race, with Lecornu and Geffray still managing residual blockades.

        1. RSF seizes El Fasher; mass killings shock Darfur

        Qué pasó

        Between 26–30 October 2025, Sudan’s Rapid Support Forces overran El Fasher—the army’s last major holdout in Darfur—after an 18-month siege. UN and rights groups soon documented summary executions, hospital massacres (WHO cited hundreds killed at Saudi Hospital), sexual violence and abduction-for-ransom; OHCHR later estimated more than 6,000 killed in three days. BBC Verify geolocated execution videos; Yale’s Humanitarian Research Lab flagged satellite clusters consistent with bodies in the streets. The fall cemented RSF control of almost all of Darfur and raised genocide alarms.

        Sources: BBC, BBC (hospital toll), Reuters, AP, Human Rights Watch (one-year mark)

        Conexión con hoy

        One year later (Oct–Nov 2026), El Fasher remains an RSF-held ghost town—population collapsed from over a million toward tens of thousands. Amnesty and HRW report ongoing detention, torture and blocked aid; UN investigators say the campaign bore “hallmarks of genocide.” RSF leader Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo (“Hemedti”) has largely escaped new accountability; early casualty and atrocity reporting has been reinforced, not retracted, by subsequent OHCHR and HRW findings.

          2. Trump and Xi strike a one-year trade truce in Busan

          Qué pasó

          On 30 October 2025, on the sidelines of APEC in Busan, South Korea, Donald Trump and Xi Jinping held their first face-to-face meeting since 2019 and announced a roughly one-year trade truce: the U.S. cut fentanyl-linked tariffs (overall China rate from about 57% to 47%); China paused newly announced rare-earth export curbs, resumed U.S. soybean purchases, and pledged tighter fentanyl-precursor enforcement. Analysts called it a tactical freeze, not a structural reset; Trump billed the sit-down a “12 out of 10.”

          Sources: Al Jazeera, AP, Reuters, AP (takeaways)

          Conexión con hoy

          The political truce was later extended toward 10 January 2027, and a separate 2026 Washington summit built on it—but China’s rare-earth legal pause was still set to expire around 10 November 2026 unless Beijing issued a fresh notice, leaving markets jittery as of digest date. Early skepticism that Busan was a “partial freeze” proved right: tariffs stayed high, tech controls remained contested, and rare earths stayed leverage. Distinct from any later Xi state visit to Washington.

            3. Typhoon Kalmaegi (Tino) devastates the Philippines, then Vietnam

            Qué pasó

            On 4 November 2025, Typhoon Kalmaegi—locally Tino—made landfall in the central Philippines (Southern Leyte/Cebu and Visayas), dumping roughly a month’s rain in a day, sweeping cars through Cebu streets and obliterating poorer neighbourhoods. By 7 November, the BBC put the Philippine death toll near 188 (later tallies climbed higher as twin storms were counted) with well over 100 missing; the storm then hit central Vietnam, adding further deaths and mass evacuations before weakening toward Cambodia and Laos. Manila declared a state of calamity as another Pacific storm loomed.

            Sources: BBC, AP, ReliefWeb / OCHA, Rappler (one-year recovery)

            Conexión con hoy

            A year on, Cebu and Negros Occidental are still rebuilding roads, schools and flood defences; Rappler and local press report families still in tents and incomplete resettlement. Early death-toll figures rose as body recovery continued—standard for major Asian typhoons—but the core story of catastrophic flash floods and climate-amplified rainfall held. Kalmaegi/Tino remains a benchmark disaster for Philippine disaster policy and Visayas reconstruction into late 2026.

              1. Peng Shuai vanishes after accusing a Chinese official

              Qué pasó

              On 2 November 2021, Chinese tennis star Peng Shuai posted on Weibo accusing former vice-premier Zhang Gaoli of sexual assault; censors scrubbed the post within minutes, and she disappeared from independent public view. The Women’s Tennis Association demanded proof she was free to speak and a transparent investigation—turning a #MeToo allegation into a global sports-and-diplomacy crisis ahead of the Beijing Winter Olympics. Stars including Serena Williams and Naomi Osaka amplified #WhereIsPengShuai as state media circulated contested “she’s fine” messages the WTA rejected.

              Sources: The Guardian, BBC Sport (WTA suspension), WTA (Steve Simon statement), AP (boycott ends)

              Conexión con hoy

              On 1 December 2021 the WTA suspended all tournaments in China and Hong Kong; it resumed play in China in autumn 2023 after saying it had assurances Peng was safe and living with family in Beijing, without securing the independent investigation it had demanded. As of late 2025–2026, reporting indicates she lives quietly in Beijing and has been seen in private settings, but she has not freely addressed the allegations in open media, and Zhang Gaoli has never publicly answered them. The episode remains a landmark of how sports governing bodies can—and sometimes later walk back—confronting authoritarian censorship.

                2. Belarus pushes migrants onto Poland’s frozen frontier

                Qué pasó

                Through early–mid November 2021—especially from about 8–18 November—thousands of migrants, largely from the Middle East and Afghanistan, were stranded in freezing forest strips along the Belarus–Poland border. The EU accused Alexander Lukashenko’s government of deliberately funneling people westward as retaliation for sanctions after the disputed 2020 election and the Ryanair forced landing; Poland massed troops, built barriers, and used water cannon and tear gas as deaths and hypothermia cases mounted. Russia flew nuclear-capable bombers over Belarus and sent troops for drills, while Warsaw framed the crisis as a “hybrid attack” with a Moscow shadow.

                Sources: Al Jazeera explainer (12 Nov 2021), NPR, Human Rights Watch report (24 Nov 2021), BBC — Poland water cannon / tear gas

                Conexión con hoy

                Early reporting that Minsk was instrumentalizing migration held up; repatriation flights and temporary shelters later eased the immediate crush, but the tactic entered Europe’s security playbook. Into 2025–2026 Poland has repeatedly extended border exclusion zones and asylum restrictions despite fewer illegal crossings, while limited Belarus–Poland contacts (prisoner swaps, exploratory talks) coexist with Lukashenko still in power and migration pressure remaining a lever. The episode hardened EU external-border politics and foreshadowed the broader Russia–West confrontation that exploded weeks later over Ukraine.

                  3. NATO warns as Russia masses troops near Ukraine

                  Qué pasó

                  In early–mid November 2021, Ukraine said roughly 90,000–nearly 100,000 Russian troops remained near its borders after exercises; on 15 November NATO Secretary-General Jens Stoltenberg called the concentration “large and unusual” and warned Moscow against aggressive action. U.S. and European officials privately briefed allies on intelligence suggesting a possible invasion plan, while the Kremlin dismissed the alarm as “alarmist” and pointed to NATO activity in the Black Sea. The buildup sat alongside the Belarus border crisis, which some Western officials saw as distraction or pressure in the same theater.

                  Sources: BBC — NATO warning (15 Nov 2021), BBC — why Moscow is stoking tensions, BBC — view from Ukraine front line, AP — war by the numbers / later updates

                  Conexión con hoy

                  The warnings were not overblown: Russia launched a full-scale invasion on 24 February 2022. By late 2025–November 2026 the war is a grinding multi-year conflict—Russia holding roughly a fifth of Ukraine’s territory, casualties on both sides in the high hundreds of thousands, and reconstruction needs estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars—while U.S.-mediated talks and debates over postwar security guarantees continue. Volodymyr Zelenskyy remains Ukraine’s president; Vladimir Putin remains Russia’s. The November 2021 force posture is now read as the visible overture to Europe’s largest land war since 1945.

                      2. Standing Rock: water cannons in sub-freezing Dakota night

                      Qué pasó

                      On the night of 20–21 November 2016, roughly 400 “water protectors” opposing the Dakota Access Pipeline clashed with law enforcement at Backwater Bridge near the Standing Rock Sioux camps in North Dakota. Authorities used tear gas, rubber bullets, and continuous water spray in temperatures around 23°F (−5°C); medics reported more than 150 injuries and widespread hypothermia, while police called it a riot and denied using “water cannons” even as video showed sustained streams on crowds. The standoff was then the largest Native-led environmental protest movement in modern U.S. history, focused on treaty land, sacred sites, and drinking water from the Missouri River.

                      Sources: NBC News — protesters soaked in freezing temperatures (20–21 Nov 2016), CBC — protesters repelled at bridge, Harvard EELP DAPL tracker (updated Oct 2026), The Guardian — Army Corps camp closure order

                      Conexión con hoy

                      After Obama’s Army Corps briefly denied the Lake Oahe easement in December 2016, the first Trump administration approved it in early 2017; oil has flowed for years while courts ordered fuller environmental review. On 21 May 2026 the Corps signed a Record of Decision granting a new easement with added leak-detection and monitoring conditions; on 9 October 2026 the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe filed a fresh federal suit seeking to vacate that decision and shut the line down. The 2016 camps are gone, but DAPL remains both operating infrastructure and unfinished legal-political conflict—and a template for later pipeline and Indigenous-rights fights.

                        3. Fidel Castro dies at 90

                        Qué pasó

                        On the evening of 25 November 2016 (announced overnight into the 26th), Cuban President Raúl Castro told the nation on state TV that his brother Fidel—revolutionary commander, prime minister, then president who ruled Cuba for nearly five decades—had died at age 90. Nine days of mourning followed; ashes were carried across the island to Santiago. Admirers hailed anti-imperial defiance and social gains; critics damned a one-party dictatorship marked by prisons, exile, and suppressed speech. The death came just after Trump’s U.S. election, raising immediate questions about the fragile Obama-era thaw with Havana.

                        Sources: BBC — Fidel Castro dies at 90, Reuters — Cuban revolutionary leader dies aged 90, CNN obituary / video package, AP — Castro family still central to Cuba’s leadership

                        Conexión con hoy

                        Fidel’s death did not liberalize Cuba’s political system. Raúl Castro left the presidency in 2018 (Miguel Díaz-Canel succeeded him and later took the Communist Party leadership), but the Castro family and military-party apparatus remain central. The island has endured deepening economic crisis, mass emigration, and tightened U.S. pressure across administrations—including Trump’s second term. A decade on, Fidel’s personal cult has faded into history while the revolutionary state’s structures—and the unresolved U.S.–Cuba estrangement—persist.
