1. Serbia's student movement ends Vučić's 14-year grip on power

Qué pasó

On 25 October 2026, Serbia's Student List won a snap parliamentary election outright, ending the rule of Aleksandar Vučić's Serbian Progressive Party (SNS), which had been in power since 2012. The movement grew out of the November 2024 collapse of a station canopy in Novi Sad that killed 16 people, a disaster protesters blamed on corruption and negligence. After nearly two years of street protests, Vučić dissolved parliament on 9 September and resigned as president on 27 September so he could lead the SNS list and try to return as prime minister. It did not work. Final results from the electoral commission gave the Student List 53.69% and 140 of 250 seats, against 39.48% and 103 seats for Vučić's "United Serbia" list. Turnout was 68.89%, about ten points higher than in 2023. Vučić conceded on election night: "It is clear we have lost these elections." Crowds celebrated in Belgrade, Novi Sad and other cities. The Student List had no formal leader. It ran doctors, actors, academics and athletes, headed by Ilija Srdanović, on a programme of purging officials who cracked down on protesters and freeing the public broadcaster from political control.

Sources: BBC: Vučić concedes election to student-led protest movement, Wikipedia: 2026 Serbian parliamentary election (final RIK results), Srpske Novine: Čupić sets presidential election for 27 December, Srpske Novine: Vučić sees no point in SNS running for president

Conexión con hoy

The result has held, but the new majority is moving slowly. The new parliament elected Aleksandar Čupić of the Student List as speaker with 140 votes, which also makes him acting president. He says he will call the presidential election by 26 November for a vote on 27 December. The Student List has still not named a candidate for president or for prime minister, and Čupić says a good choice matters more than a fast one. He also plans to cut the number of ministries. Vučić now sits in parliament as head of the SNS group. He calls himself "an ordinary citizen" and says it is "pointless" for the SNS to run in the presidential race. Since the vote, managers at the public broadcasters have resigned and officials and businesspeople linked to the SNS have been arrested. The EU's enlargement commissioner said "the door to the EU is open." The Kremlin praised Vučić and reminded the newcomers that "it takes two to tango." The student movement has said Serbia will still not sanction Russia.

    2. Netanyahu loses his majority in Israel's election

    Qué pasó

    Israelis voted on 27 October 2026 in the first general election since the 7 October 2023 attack and the Gaza war. Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud stayed the largest party with 27 of 120 seats, ahead of former army chief Gadi Eisenkot's new Yashar party with 21. But the blocs were what mattered. Netanyahu's camp won 53 seats, eight short of a majority. The Zionist opposition led by Eisenkot won 48, and the Arab-led parties, Ra'am and the Joint List, won a record 19. Turnout was 77.98%, the highest since 1999. As the final results came in, Likud claimed without evidence that the Arab-led parties had "stolen" the election, possibly with foreign help. The opposition accused Netanyahu of trying to undermine the vote. On 4 November the Central Elections Committee certified the results. It said a review of 9,069 polling stations found no irregularities that changed the outcome. Its chairman, Supreme Court Justice Noam Sohlberg, said those casting doubt on the results posed "a real danger" to Israeli democracy.

    Sources: Israel Central Elections Committee: final results, AP: Netanyahu's party claims election "stolen" as results show him losing majority, Al Jazeera: Results confirmed as election commission rejects fraud claims, JTA: Final results and who will form the government, Al Jazeera: Likud and Yashar end coalition talks without deal (22 Nov 2026)

    Conexión con hoy

    Israel still has no new government, and Netanyahu remains caretaker prime minister. On 8 November President Isaac Herzog gave Eisenkot the first chance to form a coalition after 67 lawmakers, including the Arab parties, recommended him. United Torah Judaism, which ran inside Netanyahu's bloc, recommended no one. Eisenkot has until 6 December, and Herzog can extend that to 20 December. On 22 November, Likud and Yashar negotiators talked about a unity government for more than four hours in Herzliya and ended without a deal. The sticking points are who serves as prime minister first and whether far-right minister Itamar Ben-Gvir is included. Eisenkot opposes a rotation, and Avigdor Lieberman refuses to sit with Netanyahu at all. If unity talks fail, Yashar plans to seek a confidence vote for a minority government backed from outside by the Arab parties. That would be a first in Israel. If nobody succeeds, Israel heads to another election early next year.

      3. North Korean hackers steal $6.3 billion from Binance

      Qué pasó

      On 29 October 2026, attackers drained about $6.3–6.4 billion in crypto from hot wallets at Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange. It is the largest crypto theft on record, roughly four times the $1.5 billion stolen from Bybit in February 2025. According to blockchain analysts at TRM Labs, most of the money left across ten blockchains in about 20 minutes, starting at 20:05 UTC. Binance co-CEO Richard Teng said the attack started with a DevOps engineer's compromised laptop, which gave the attacker access to a service container. From there the attacker submitted unauthorized withdrawals. Binance said it found no evidence that wallet private keys had leaked, and it later reported "additional points of breach." TRM Labs and Chainalysis attributed the theft to TraderTraitor, the North Korean state hacking unit also known as Lazarus Group. Chainalysis said it watched Binance funds mix on-chain with money stolen from Bitget in September. The attacker raced to swap freezable stablecoins for ether and bitcoin before issuers could block them. Tether, Circle and others froze about $2.7 billion within days. Binance halted withdrawals, and bitcoin fell more than 4%.

      Sources: BBC: Binance hit by $6.3bn hack suspected to be largest in history, BleepingComputer: Binance links theft to compromised DevOps laptop, Chainalysis: Tracing the North Korea link, CoinDesk: Binance wins $6.5 billion freezing order (19 Nov 2026), The Crypto Times: Recovery reaches just $32M after three weeks

      Conexión con hoy

      Binance says customers will not lose money. It has committed more than $6.34 billion from its user emergency fund (SAFU), futures insurance fund and corporate treasury, and on 12 November said every affected asset had been replaced. Withdrawals reopened in phases from 1 November, and the full post-mortem it promised has not yet been published. Getting the stolen money back is a separate and slower effort. By early November about $2.8 billion was frozen. On 19 November an Abu Dhabi court gave Binance a worldwide order freezing up to $6.5 billion in proceeds, and on 1 December it will consider whether 18 firms must hand over what they hold. The U.S. Justice Department says it has court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion. Even so, an on-chain investigator estimated on 21 November that only $32 million had actually been recovered, while about $2.67 billion remained in wallets the attackers control. That money is being moved through THORChain and CoinJoin mixing. Western agencies say thefts like this help pay for North Korea's weapons programmes. Pyongyang has denied involvement in past hacks.

        1. Fire tears through Hong Kong's Wang Fuk Court and kills 168 people

        Qué pasó

        On the afternoon of 26 November 2025, a fire broke out at Wang Fuk Court, a housing estate of eight high-rise towers in Tai Po, Hong Kong, home to nearly 4,800 people. The estate was under major renovation and wrapped in bamboo scaffolding, green mesh and foam boards over the windows. Flames raced up the facades and jumped from tower to tower until seven of the eight were burning. It was the city's deadliest fire in decades. Early tests found that some of the mesh failed fire-retardant standards. Residents had complained about the netting for almost a year, and the Labour Department admitted it had carried out 16 inspections and issued warnings to contractors since July 2024. Police arrested 13 people on suspicion of manslaughter within days, and the anti-corruption commission (ICAC) opened its own inquiry into the renovation contract. The government also took a harder line on criticism. National-security officials warned of "evil schemes" to exploit the disaster, and police arrested at least three people, including a petition organizer, on suspicion of sedition.

        Sources: The Independent: Mesh and polystyrene helped spread the fire (Dec 2025), HK Government: Seven individuals and two companies charged (10 Jun 2026), Independent Committee report, Volume 1, news.gov.hk: Wang Fuk Court fire report released (29 Oct 2026), China Daily HK: Post-fire support and reform progress (24 Nov 2026)

        Conexión con hoy

        The final death toll is 168, including one firefighter. Nearly 2,000 families lost their homes. A judge-led independent committee reported in October 2026. It found the fire was most likely started by a glowing source, probably a cigarette, on a flat roof. It said nets were swapped for non-fire-retardant ones, flammable foam boards were used, and openings were cut into escape staircases, and that this is why the fire spread so fast and killed so many. It also described bid-rigging syndicates linked to triads and made 120 recommendations. On 29 October Chief Executive John Lee and his officials bowed in apology, and Housing Secretary Winnie Ho resigned. In June, seven people and two companies, the consultant Will Power and the contractor Prestige, were charged with 25 offences, including five counts of manslaughter and fraud over how the contract was won. Their trials are pending. A new anti-bid-rigging task force has arrested 163 people. About 84% of owners have now sold their flats to the government, but only 15 households have the keys to new homes. The anniversary is the day after tomorrow.

          2. Bolsonaro is jailed after taking a soldering iron to his ankle monitor

          Qué pasó

          Early on 22 November 2025, Brazil's Federal Police arrested former president Jair Bolsonaro at his home in Brasília. He had been under house arrest while appealing his September conviction for plotting a coup after losing the 2022 election. Supreme Court Justice Alexandre de Moraes ordered the arrest because the ankle monitor had been tampered with overnight, and because his son, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, had called supporters to a vigil outside the house. The court said that meant a "high possibility of an attempted escape." In a video released by the court, Bolsonaro admits using a "hot iron" on the device. He said he did it out of "curiosity," and later blamed medication-induced "paranoia." On 25 November, Moraes declared the case closed with no appeals left and ordered him to begin serving his sentence of 27 years and three months in a Federal Police cell. Generals Augusto Heleno and Paulo Sérgio Nogueira, both former ministers, were also sent to prison. The Trump administration called the arrest "provocative and unnecessary."

          Sources: CNN: Bolsonaro arrested to prevent "attempted escape" (22 Nov 2025), BBC: Bolsonaro ordered to start serving 27-year sentence, Euronews: Bolsonaro in intensive care (21 Nov 2026), Agência Brasil: Bolsonaro diagnosed with pneumonia (23 Nov 2026)

          Conexión con hoy

          Bolsonaro, 71, is still serving his sentence, but at home. In March 2026 Moraes granted him "humanitarian" house arrest after a bout of bronchopneumonia. On 20 November 2026 he was taken to hospital with an intestinal obstruction, and on 23 November doctors at DF Star hospital in Brasília said he had bilateral aspiration pneumonia. He is stable in intensive care with no discharge date. His son Carlos says his health is "deteriorating day by day." The Supreme Court is due to rule in December on a law that would cut the sentences of Bolsonaro and the others convicted over the coup plot. The family's political comeback also failed. Flávio lost the presidential runoff to Lula on 25 October, Moraes then ordered his passports seized in a money-laundering investigation, and he is now in the United States and still refuses to concede. Bolsonaro remains barred from public office until 2060.

            3. Two National Guard members are shot near the White House

            Qué pasó

            On 26 November 2025, the day before Thanksgiving, a gunman ambushed West Virginia National Guard members outside the Farragut West metro station, two blocks from the White House. They were in Washington as part of Trump's deployment of troops to the capital. Specialist Sarah Beckstrom, 20, and Air Force Staff Sergeant Andrew Wolfe, 24, were both shot in the head. Two Guard majors subdued and wounded the attacker. Beckstrom died the next day. The suspect was Rahmanullah Lakanwal, 29, an Afghan who had served in a CIA-backed "Zero Unit" strike force in Kandahar. He came to the U.S. in 2021 under Operation Allies Welcome and was granted asylum in April 2025. Investigators say he drove across the country from Bellingham, Washington, with a revolver stolen in Seattle. The administration blamed Biden-era vetting and responded within hours. It ordered 500 more troops to Washington and halted all immigration applications from Afghans. USCIS stopped all asylum decisions and ordered a review of green cards issued to people from 19 "countries of concern." Trump promised to "permanently pause migration from all Third World Countries."

            Sources: Wikipedia: 2025 Washington, D.C., National Guard shooting, AP: US halts all asylum decisions after the shooting, FOX 5 DC: Suspect pleads not guilty, faces death penalty (Jun 2026), NBC News/AP: Judge strikes down policy freezing immigration from 39 countries (Jun 2026), Reuters: USCIS resumes asylum claims after halt (Mar 2026)

            Conexión con hoy

            Wolfe survived and learned to stand and breathe on his own again. At the February 2026 State of the Union, Trump awarded him the Purple Heart, and gave one posthumously to Beckstrom. Lakanwal has pleaded not guilty to a 17-count federal indictment, some of whose charges carry the death penalty. The Justice Department has not said whether it will seek execution, and no trial date has been set. During a hunger strike this year, a federal judge allowed him to be force-fed. Many of the immigration measures did not survive in their original form. USCIS began resuming asylum decisions in March 2026, according to Reuters. On 5 June a federal judge in Boston struck down the policy that had frozen benefit decisions for immigrants from 39 countries, calling the national-security reasons "pretextual." The shooting still shaped policy, though: it was the starting point for a wider travel ban and for re-reviews of green cards and asylum grants already approved.

              1. South Africa flags a new variant, and the world meets Omicron

              Qué pasó

              On 25 November 2021, South African scientists announced that they had found a heavily mutated coronavirus variant, B.1.1.529. It had first been detected in Botswana on 11 November, carried 32 mutations in its spike protein (about twice as many as Delta) and was spreading fast in Gauteng province. Genomics expert Tulio de Oliveira's team moved quickly and shared the data openly. On 26 November, the World Health Organization declared it a "variant of concern" and named it Omicron. The world reacted fast and harshly. Britain, the EU, the United States and others banned travel from South Africa and its neighbours, even though the WHO warned against knee-jerk travel restrictions. Markets fell sharply. The Dow lost 905 points on 26 November, its worst day of the year. South African officials said they were being "punished" for doing good science, and scientists warned that the bans would discourage other countries from reporting new variants.

              Sources: WHO: Classification of Omicron as a variant of concern (26 Nov 2021), CNBC: Dow falls 900 points for worst day of year, NYT: South Africa feels "punished" by travel bans, The Guardian: Scientists warn of new variant with high number of mutations (24 Nov 2021), Gavi VaccinesWork: The XFG variant (Oct 2026), Folha: XFG becomes dominant in Brazil (Nov 2026)

              Conexión con hoy

              The early alarm was justified on transmission and immune escape, and less so on severity. Omicron spread around the world within weeks and set record case numbers in early 2022. It was generally milder than Delta, but it still killed many people because so many were infected. Its descendants have caused almost every COVID infection since 2022. The variant most common worldwide today, XFG ("Stratus"), is an Omicron sub-lineage. It made up about 49% of shared sequences in September 2026, and it is behind a current rise in cases in Brazil. The WHO lists it only as a "variant under monitoring," with no evidence of more severe disease. Vaccines are now updated for Omicron sub-lineages: the WHO's advisers recommended LP.8.1 in May 2026. South Africa's lesson still stands: scientists warned at the time that punishing a country for sharing data would make others think twice about reporting the next variant.

                2. The deadliest small-boat sinking in the Channel

                Qué pasó

                In the early hours of 24 November 2021, an overloaded inflatable dinghy that had left northern France for England sank in the Channel. At least 27 people died and four were never found. Only two men survived. Most of the victims were Iraqi Kurds. Others came from Afghanistan, Ethiopia, Iran, Somalia, Vietnam and Egypt, and the youngest known victim was a seven-year-old girl. Over several hours the passengers made about a dozen calls for help to French and British rescue services as the boat deflated. Nearly 12 hours after the first call, a French fishing boat found the bodies. It was the worst loss of life in the Channel since small-boat crossings began. Britain and France blamed each other. Boris Johnson published a letter to Emmanuel Macron, and France responded by withdrawing Home Secretary Priti Patel's invitation to a migration meeting in Calais.

                Sources: The Guardian: Loss of life was avoidable, Cranston inquiry finds (Feb 2026), GOV.UK: The Report of the Cranston Inquiry, The Guardian: Priti Patel disinvited from meeting with France (Nov 2021), The Guardian: French court jails six men for manslaughter (Sep 2026), Eurojust: Thirteen convicted for smuggling operation

                Conexión con hoy

                The two investigations ended this year, and both found that the deaths were avoidable. In February 2026, the UK's Cranston Inquiry concluded that at least 33 people had been aboard. It found that a chronically understaffed HM Coastguard had confused the dinghy with another boat and called off the search, while people were still alive in the water. As many as 15 victims were probably still alive at sunrise. It also found that the French naval vessel Flamant, the closest ship, never responded to the mayday: "If the Flamant had attended… many more and possibly all lives would have been saved." On 30 September 2026 a Paris court jailed six smugglers for manslaughter, for six to ten years, and convicted seven others of lesser offences. Seven French military personnel remain under investigation for failing to help people in danger, which they deny. The crossings continue. Families are still waiting for the bodies of the four missing.

                  3. Germany's "traffic light" coalition is born

                  Qué pasó

                  On 24 November 2021, after a month of negotiations, Olaf Scholz's Social Democrats, the Greens and the liberal Free Democrats (FDP) presented a 177-page coalition agreement titled "Dare more progress." It was Germany's first three-party "traffic light" government at federal level, and it ended 16 years of Angela Merkel. The deal promised to raise the minimum wage to €12, bring the coal exit forward "ideally" to 2030, put 15 million electric cars on the road by 2030, build 400,000 homes a year, legalize cannabis sales to adults, lower the voting age to 16, and return to the constitutional debt brake from 2023. "The traffic light is on," Scholz said. Greens leader Robert Habeck admitted the talks had been "sometimes quite exhausting." FDP leader Christian Lindner presented it as a change of course. Scholz was elected chancellor on 8 December 2021.

                  Sources: tagesschau: "Die Ampel steht" (24 Nov 2021), Wikipedia: Scholz cabinet, abgeordnetenwatch: What the traffic-light ministers do now, tagesschau: Scholz to chair the North–South Commission (Jun 2026)

                  Conexión con hoy

                  The coalition delivered some of its promises: the €12 minimum wage in October 2022, partial cannabis legalization in 2024 and a new citizenship law. But Russia's invasion of Ukraine, the energy crisis and constant fights over money wore it down. On 6 November 2024 Scholz fired Lindner as finance minister over the budget, and the FDP left the government. Scholz lost a confidence vote on 16 December. In the snap election of 23 February 2025, the SPD fell to its worst result in its post-war history and the FDP dropped out of the Bundestag. Friedrich Merz of the CDU became chancellor on 6 May 2025, leading a coalition with the SPD. All three leaders are alive. Scholz, 68, is a backbench MP for Potsdam and a partner in a Hamburg law firm, and since June 2026 he has co-chaired a new government-appointed North–South Commission on development. Habeck works as a foreign-policy analyst in Copenhagen. Lindner has gone into business: since January 2026 he has been deputy chair of the management board of car dealer Autoland AG.

                    1. Jill Stein forces recounts in Wisconsin, Michigan and Pennsylvania

                    Qué pasó

                    On 23 November 2016, Green Party candidate Jill Stein started raising money for recounts in Wisconsin, Michigan and Pennsylvania. Donald Trump had won the three states by fewer than 80,000 votes combined. Some computer scientists had urged audits to rule out hacking or machine errors, though they offered no evidence that either had happened. Stein raised more than $7 million in days, far more than her own campaign had. She filed in Wisconsin on 25 November, and on 26 November the Clinton campaign said it would take part, while stressing that it had found no evidence of tampering. Trump called the effort "a scam" and claimed, without evidence, that he had lost the popular vote only because "millions of people" voted illegally.

                    Sources: AP: Recount efforts end: Trump wins in Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, NPR: Clinton campaign says it will participate in recount efforts, CBS News: Trump calls recount a Green Party "scam", AP: Trump celebrates adding 131 votes in Wisconsin, NYT: Stein ends recount bid but says it revealed flaws, St. Louis Magazine: Stein demands jury trial (Sep 2026), CounterPunch: trial date to be set 25 January 2027 (Nov 2026)

                    Conexión con hoy

                    The recounts confirmed Trump's wins. Wisconsin's full recount ended on 12 December 2016 and added 131 votes to Trump's margin, which stood at 22,748. A federal judge stopped Michigan's recount after three days, and Pennsylvania's request was rejected. Stein raised about $7.33 million from roughly 161,000 donors and said the effort had exposed weaknesses in voting systems, such as unbalanced precinct counts in Detroit. The episode was an early sign of how losing sides now fight elections after the fact. In 2020 it was Trump who paid for a partial Wisconsin recount, which added to Biden's lead, and who went on to make false fraud claims much larger than Stein's. Stein, 76, ran for president again in 2024. She now faces a misdemeanour assault and trespass case over a 2024 pro-Palestinian protest at Washington University in St. Louis. She rejected a deal to drop the charges in exchange for community service and is asking for a jury trial, and a trial date is expected to be set on 25 January 2027.

                      2. Wildfire sweeps out of the Smokies into Gatlinburg

                      Qué pasó

                      On the evening of 23 November 2016, a small fire was reported on the Chimney Tops in Great Smoky Mountains National Park, during the worst drought East Tennessee had seen in years. For five days it smouldered on cliffs that crews could not safely reach, and park officials judged it no threat to nearby towns. On 28 November, winds gusting up to 87 mph threw embers miles ahead and brought down power lines. The fire left the park at 6:08 p.m. and swept into Gatlinburg and the rest of Sevier County, where it merged with other fires. Thousands of people fled down mountain roads through the flames. Some never got evacuation alerts because cell towers were down, and the state emergency agency did not send a mass alert. Fourteen people died, about 190 were injured and nearly 2,500 buildings were destroyed, with damage of around $2 billion. It was Tennessee's largest documented wildfire. Two teenagers were charged with aggravated arson.

                      Sources: WVLT: Federal judge dismisses Gatlinburg wildfire lawsuits (Apr 2026), WBIR: 10 years later, remembering the 2016 Gatlinburg wildfires, Visit Gatlinburg & Pigeon Forge: Timeline and 10th-anniversary remembrance

                      Conexión con hoy

                      Prosecutors dropped the charges against the two teenagers in June 2017. They cited the hurricane-force winds and other ignition points, such as downed power lines. The park later spent about $2.5 million upgrading its communications. Dolly Parton, who grew up in Sevier County, set up the My People Fund, which gave more than 900 families who lost their homes $10,000 each. Her telethon raised $9 million. The victims' lawsuits against the federal government have gone nowhere. On 31 March 2026 a federal judge dismissed 11 consolidated cases. He ruled that the park's warning decisions, "while far from perfect," were protected judgment calls. Gatlinburg has been rebuilt and the forests are green again, but the last section of the Chimney Tops Trail is still closed because the burned summit is unstable. A ceremony at the memorial in Mynatt Park will mark the 10th anniversary this Saturday, 28 November.

                        3. The European Parliament votes to freeze Turkey's EU membership talks

                        Qué pasó

                        On 24 November 2016, the European Parliament voted by 479 to 37, with 107 abstentions, to urge a "temporary freeze" of Turkey's EU accession talks. The reason was President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's crackdown after the failed coup in July. By then about 120,000 people had been dismissed or suspended from public jobs and some 40,000 arrested, journalists had been rounded up and pro-Kurdish MPs detained. The resolution was non-binding, and EU foreign-policy chief Federica Mogherini warned against it. The EU depended on Turkey's March 2016 deal to hold back migrants. Erdoğan called the vote "worthless." The next day he threatened Europe directly: "If you go any further, these border gates will be opened." Talks had started in 2005, but only one of 35 negotiating chapters had been closed.

                        Sources: BBC: Euro MPs vote to freeze Turkey EU membership talks, European Parliament press release (24 Nov 2016), Reuters: Erdoğan warns Europe Turkey could open migrant gates, European Commission: Türkiye 2026 Report, Anadolu: EU's Türkiye report amid stalled talks (Nov 2026), BBC Türkçe: What is in the EU's Turkey report? (Nov 2026)

                        Conexión con hoy

                        The vote was a sign of where things were heading. EU governments never formally suspended talks, but no new chapter has been opened since 2016, and accession negotiations have been "at a standstill" since June 2018. The Commission repeated that in its 4 November 2026 report on Turkey. In 2017 Turks voted to create an executive presidency, and in 2020 Erdoğan briefly carried out his threat by waving migrants toward the Greek border. The report says the gap between Turkey's EU ambitions and its democratic record has widened. It points out that Istanbul mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu, the opposition's strongest presidential hopeful, remains in detention, and that his corruption trial, which began on 9 March 2026, carries a possible sentence of up to 2,430 years. Ankara called the report "biased." The relationship now runs on interests, not membership: the EU has spent about €12.5 billion on refugees in Turkey since 2011 and treats Turkey, with NATO's second-largest army, as a key security and defence partner. Erdoğan, 72, is still president.
